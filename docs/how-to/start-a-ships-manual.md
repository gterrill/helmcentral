# Start a ship's manual

The hard part of writing a manual for your boat isn't the writing. It's
deciding what goes in it, and that decision is what stops most people before
they have a page. This is a starting structure and an argument about what
belongs where. For how to capture, file and order the sections themselves,
see [Notes and the manual](../features/notes-and-the-manual.md) and
[Write the boat's manual](write-the-boats-manual.md).

One piece of advice comes before the structure, because it matters more than
the structure does: **do not sit down to write a manual.** Capture notes as
things happen over a season, then file them into sections once you have enough
of them to see the shape. Putting a manual together out of notes you've already
written is a different job from writing one from nothing, and only one of those
jobs ever gets finished.

## Two manuals, not one

Boats that do this well usually end up with more than one, because the
audiences are different and mixing them makes both worse.

There are two kinds:

- **Operations manual:** This one is for whoever is running the boat,
  including you at 0300 when something is wrong. It's mostly procedures and
  checklists.
- **Crew training manual:** This one is for teaching someone who doesn't yet
  know the boat. It covers the same systems but explains them, whereas the
  operations manual assumes you already understand them and just need the
  sequence.

If you only write one, write the operations manual. You'll use it more, and a
guest can follow a good checklist without understanding the system behind it.

## A reference spine

The structure below is adapted from a standard yacht owner's manual proforma.
It's worth having for two reasons. It's the shape surveyors, brokers and the
next owner expect, and working through it brings up systems you'd forgotten you
own.

1. **Introduction.** What the boat is, and who this manual is for.
2. **General information and craft data.** Dimensions, tank capacities,
   engine and electrical data, hull construction, the general arrangement and
   the engine room layout.
3. **Installations.** Engine, propulsion, generator, fuel, cooling and
   exhaust, fresh water, grey and black water, sea water and through-hulls.
   Also electrical, ventilation, heating and air conditioning, bilge pumping,
   steering, hydraulics, thrusters, deck wash, navigation and
   communications.
4. **Deck equipment.** Winches, anchoring, navigation lights, boarding
   ladder, fenders, mooring lines, tender stowage.
5. **Safety.** Flooding and stability, mooring, towing, firefighting,
   liferafts, man overboard, first aid.
6. **Maintenance.** Lifting, winter storage, painting.
7. **Annexes.** Wiring diagrams, schedules, anything that is a drawing rather
   than a paragraph.

## What that spine does not cover

That structure describes what the boat **is**. It's a builder's document, and
your boat almost certainly came with one already.

An operations manual describes how **this** boat is run. That's a different
thing, and it's mostly missing from the builder's version. It's the layer you
have to write yourself, because nobody else knows it:

- **Starting up and shutting down.** The actual sequence, in order, including
  the steps that only become obvious once you've got them wrong.
- **Leaving the dock, and coming back to it.** Which spring goes on first,
  which way she backs, and what the thruster will and won't do in a
  crosswind.
- **Anchoring.** Scope for the ground you usually anchor in, how much chain
  is marked and where, what the windlass does when the breaker trips.
- **Fuelling and fuel transfer.** Which valve is supply and which is return,
  and which tank the transfer pump actually draws from.
- **Leaving the boat.** For a weekend, and for a month. These are different
  lists and both are easy to get wrong from memory.
- **When it won't start.** The genset, the main, the watermaker. What you
  check, in the order you check it.

This layer is where checklists live, and checklists are the part of a manual
that gets used most. Underway, a procedure written as a list of ticks is worth
more than three paragraphs of prose saying the same thing.

## The quirks are the most valuable pages

The things worth writing down first are the ones that are true of your hull
and no other. Think of the previous owner's rewiring. The seacock that is stiff and
needs the handle a particular way. The gauge that reads high by a known
amount. The switch that has to be off before the boat is left for a fortnight.

None of that is in any manufacturer's book. None of it can be recovered once
you forget it. And all of it is what the next owner will most want and least
expect to get.

## How much is enough

A section that says "the fuel return is the inboard valve, the one with the
scratched handle" is finished. It doesn't need a paragraph of context. The
test is whether someone who doesn't know the boat could act on it, not whether
it reads like a publication.

Manufacturer documentation for the equipment aboard belongs in the document
library rather than in this manual, so refer to it and move on. Your manual's
job is the part that's only true here.
